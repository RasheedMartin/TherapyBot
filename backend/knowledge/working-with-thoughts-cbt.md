# Working with anxious and negative thoughts (CBT)

## The CBT model in brief
Cognitive behavioural therapy looks at how situations, thoughts, feelings, body sensations, and behaviours influence each other. The same event can feel very different depending on how we interpret it. CBT does not mean "think positive"; it means checking whether a thought is accurate and helpful, and testing beliefs through action.

## Common thinking traps
- **Catastrophizing**: jumping to the worst possible outcome.
- **Mind reading**: assuming you know what others think of you.
- **All-or-nothing thinking**: seeing things as total success or total failure.
- **Overgeneralizing**: one bad event becomes "this always happens."
- **Should statements**: rigid rules about how you or others must behave.
- **Discounting the positive**: dismissing evidence that doesn't fit a negative view.
Naming the trap ("I'm catastrophizing") often creates a little distance from the thought.

## A simple thought record
1. Situation: what happened, where, when?
2. Automatic thought: what went through your mind?
3. Feelings: name them and rate their intensity from 0 to 100.
4. Evidence for and against the thought.
5. A more balanced thought that accounts for all the evidence.
6. Re-rate the feelings.
Writing it down usually works better than doing it in your head.

## Testing beliefs with action
Avoidance keeps anxiety going because the feared outcome is never tested. In CBT, people gradually and deliberately approach feared situations, starting small (exposure), and notice what actually happens. A therapist can help design steps that are challenging but manageable.

## Rumination and worry
When thoughts loop, it can help to schedule a short daily "worry time," write worries down to postpone them until then, and ask "Is this a problem I can act on now?" If yes, plan one small next step; if not, practice letting the thought pass and refocusing on the present.
