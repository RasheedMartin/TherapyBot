# Supporting a teen who is struggling or resists help

## Lead with listening
Teens are more likely to open up when they feel heard rather than fixed. Validate the feeling ("That sounds really stressful") before offering solutions. Side-by-side moments such as car rides, cooking, or walks often feel less intense than face-to-face talks.

## When a teen refuses therapy
- Explain the reason for concern calmly, using specific observations rather than labels.
- Offer real choices: type of therapist, in person or online, gender or background of the therapist.
- Suggest a limited trial ("try three sessions, then we'll decide together").
- Frame therapy as a tool for their goals (sleeping better, less conflict, doing well at school) rather than a sign something is wrong with them.
- Parents can start their own sessions or parent coaching; family changes can help even when the teen isn't attending yet.

## Reducing family conflict
Pick calm times for important conversations, keep requests short and specific, and focus on one issue at a time. Notice and praise positive behaviour more often than you correct. Repairing after an argument ("I got too loud earlier, I'm sorry") models the skills you hope to see.

## Warning signs that need prompt help
Talk of suicide or self-harm, giving away possessions, sudden withdrawal, substance use, or major changes in eating or sleeping need prompt professional attention. If there is immediate risk, call or text 988 in the US or call emergency services.
